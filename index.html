    Cost & schedule control

    Earned Value & the S-Curve

    An S-curve compares three numbers every month: what you planned to have built (PV), what you actually built, priced at budget rates (EV), and what you actually spent (AC). Because construction ramps up slowly, peaks mid-project, and tapers at close-out, the cumulative lines form an “S”.

    • CPI = EV ÷ AC. Below 1.0 means every rand of work completed is costing more than a rand. Here: 0.95.
    • SPI = EV ÷ PV. Below 1.0 means less work is done than planned — behind schedule. Here: 0.95.
    • Forecast at completion projects today's efficiency to the end: budget ÷ CPI ≈ R 91.1M.
    Scope control

    Change Management

    No scope change is “free”. Each change order is priced for cost and schedule impact, then formally approved, kept pending for a decision, or rejected — before the work happens, not after the invoice arrives.

    • Approved changes roll into the revised budget (R 88,562,700 here).
    • Pending changes are tracked as exposure (R 1,726,200) so leadership sees the risk early.
    • Rejected changes stay on the log — the audit trail matters at close-out.
    Communication control

    RFI Tracking

    An RFI is a formal question from the builder to the design team or owner: a drawing conflict, an unclear spec, a missing detail. Each one gets an owner (“ball in court”), a due date, and a status.

    • Overdue RFIs are flagged because an unanswered question can idle a crew.
    • The log creates a paper trail: who was asked, when, and what was decided.
    • At handover, a clean RFI log is part of the close-out deliverables package.

I'm proudly pro-Black and committed to seeing Black people thrive in technical spaces, and Johannesburg's Maboneng Precinct is exactly where that commitment meets real project-controls work: a real arts district rebuilt around galleries, theatres, studios and small business. Expanding a centre there, in phases, without ever closing it — that is the human version of a hard delivery problem: every exhibition, rehearsal and performance keeps its audience while the building grows around it. The main thing about me is that I love to learn — it's how I live my life — and building this study meant learning a delivery context properly: heritage brick fabric, phased occupancy, municipal approvals, and power that keeps the show running through load-shedding. My curiosity runs wider than one lane, too: as a Mechanical & AI Systems engineer I'm drawn to mechanical design, robotics and mechatronics, automation, controls and applied AI alongside project delivery. This case study is my own work, built to show how I think — and it represents exactly the kind of project I want to deliver for real.
    About this case study. CapEx Tracker is an independent project-controls case study modeled on a real arts-district expansion in Johannesburg. The Maboneng Arts Centre is a representative project created for this study — an R 86.4M renovation and expansion in the Maboneng Precinct, delivered in phases while the centre stays open. That constraint is the central project-management challenge: work zones, safe egress, dust and noise controls and the evening programme calendar all have to be coordinated alongside cost and schedule. The scope reflects the precinct as it really is — restoration of a heritage brick façade, a performance hall with full acoustic treatment and rigging, gallery and studio space, a community kitchen, City of Johannesburg approvals, and a rooftop solar-plus-battery system so performances continue through load-shedding. All figures are illustrative estimates created for demonstration; the setting and the problem are real, but the centre, its team and its numbers are a constructed example — no client's confidential data is used or implied. The workflows mirror the capital-project delivery work I supported during my project management internship at JLL: monthly status reporting, earned-value cost control, change and RFI logs, and close-out tracking.
